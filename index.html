Why doesn't your optimizer go downhill?

You've probably heard that training a neural network means "rolling downhill" on the loss. That's only half true. Gradient descent, momentum and Adam all start from the same gradient, but they each take a step in a different direction. On this page you'll see exactly how, and you'll find a case where the "best" optimizer suddenly becomes one of the worst.

You don't need to read everything first. Every section has something to try.

1. Reading the map

Imagine the loss as a landscape: high ground means a bad model, the lowest point means the best one. We only have two parameters here, $x_1$ and $x_2$, so we can draw that landscape from above, like a hiking map.

Each ring is a contour: every point on one ring has the same loss. The X in the middle is the minimum, where we want to end up. Where rings are packed close together, the ground is steep. Where they're far apart, it's almost flat.

The landscape on this page is a long, narrow valley:

$$L(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + K\,x_2^2$$

$K$ controls how stretched the valley is. With $K = 100$, moving in $x_2$ changes the loss 100 times more than moving in $x_1$.

Try this: switch from 20:1 to 100:1. Which direction gets steeper?

What happened?

The rings squeeze together in the up-down ($x_2$) direction. Up-down is now a steep wall; left-right is a gentle slope. Real loss landscapes are full of valleys like this, and they're exactly where optimizers struggle.

2. Gradient descent: steepest is not shortest

Gradient descent does one simple thing: at the current point, find the steepest way down and take a step that way.

$$x \leftarrow x - \eta \cdot \nabla L$$

The gradient $\nabla L$ points uphill, so we step the opposite way. The learning rate $\eta$ sets how big the step is.

Here's the catch: "steepest downhill" is a local answer. In a narrow valley, the steepest direction points across the valley, straight at the nearest wall, not along the valley toward the minimum.

Try this: press Step a few times and watch the arrow. Then press Play.

What happened?

The first steps point almost straight down, across the valley, and the path bounces from side to side. Then it creeps slowly to the right toward the minimum. It takes about 240 steps.

Try this: raise the learning rate a little at a time. Watch the zig-zag grow. Then try 0.012.

What happened?

Above 0.01, gradient descent blows up: each bounce overshoots further than the last. The steep direction sets a speed limit. Go faster than it allows and you fly out of the valley. But staying under that limit means tiny steps in the flat direction, which is why gradient descent crawls.

3. Momentum: remembering where you were going

Momentum adds memory. Instead of only using today's gradient, it keeps a running velocity and adds the new gradient to it:

$$v \leftarrow \beta\, v + \nabla L \qquad x \leftarrow x - \eta \cdot v \qquad (\beta = 0.9)$$

Each step is now two pieces: a push from the new gradient and a carried-over part from earlier steps. Pushes that keep pointing the same way build up speed. Pushes that flip back and forth partly cancel out.

Try this: press Step several times and compare the two part-arrows with the step actually taken.

What happened?

Along the valley, the gradient keeps pointing the same way, so the carried-over arrow grows and the optimizer speeds up. Across the valley, the gradient flips sides, so the two parts partly cancel. Momentum reaches the minimum in about 64 steps, nearly four times faster than gradient descent.

Try this: raise the learning rate to about 0.015 and press Play.

What happened?

It swings wildly from wall to wall before settling. The carried-over arrow is now so strong that it overpowers the new gradient: the optimizer keeps going in the old direction even after the slope says turn around. Momentum doesn't remove oscillation. It changes it into big, slow swings. Push the learning rate higher and it diverges too.

4. Adam: every coordinate gets its own step size

Adam keeps two running averages for each parameter: one of the gradients ($m$), and one of the squared gradients ($s$):

$$m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1 - \beta_1)\, g \qquad s \leftarrow \beta_2 s + (1 - \beta_2)\, g^2$$

$$x \leftarrow x - \eta \cdot \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s} + \epsilon}$$

(The hats mean a small correction for the first few steps, because $m$ and $s$ start at zero.)

The key part is the division. Each coordinate is divided by its own $\sqrt{\hat s}$, the typical size of its own gradient. A coordinate with huge gradients gets divided by a big number. A coordinate with tiny gradients gets divided by a small one. So both end up taking steps of roughly the same size.

That doesn't just make the step bigger or smaller. It turns it.

A quick example: suppose the gradient is $(100, 1)$. Gradient descent steps along $-(100, 1)$, almost entirely in the steep direction. On Adam's first step, $\hat m = g$ and $\sqrt{\hat s} = |g|$ for each coordinate, so the step is $-\eta \cdot (1, 1)$: a 45° diagonal, no matter how different the two gradients were.

Try this: press Step once. Compare the three arrows.

What happened?

The gradient arrow ($-g$) points almost straight down. Adam's actual step points at 45°, moving just as much along the valley as across it. Keep stepping: as Adam's averages build up, its steps bend to follow the valley floor toward the minimum.

5. The race

Let's put all three in the same valley.

Try this: press Play.

What happened?

Adam reaches the minimum first, in about 43 steps. Momentum takes about 64, and gradient descent about 240. Adam looks like the clear winner. Remember this result.

6. The surprise: turn the valley

Here's a question to answer before you touch anything.

We'll take this exact valley, the same shape, the same steepness, the same starting point relative to the valley, and rotate it by 45°. Nothing about the problem gets harder. It's just turned.

What do you predict happens to the three optimizers?

Try this: drag Rotation to 45° and press Play.

What happened?

Gradient descent and momentum take exactly the same number of steps as before, 240 and 64. Their paths are the same paths, just rotated. Adam, the winner a moment ago, now needs about 208 steps and finishes far behind momentum.

Why? Gradient descent and momentum use the gradient as one whole arrow. Rotate the valley and the arrows rotate with it, so nothing really changes.

Adam works coordinate by coordinate, along the $x_1$ and $x_2$ axes. When the valley lines up with an axis, all the steepness sits in one coordinate and all the flatness in the other, so Adam can give each its own step size. Turned 45°, the steep direction is a mix of $x_1$ and $x_2$, and both coordinates see gradients of about the same size. Adam divides both by about the same number, its advantage disappears, and its steps point across the valley instead of along it.

Check it yourself: switch to the Adam-only view in the playground and look at the effective learning rate per coordinate. At 0° the two numbers are very different. At 45° they're almost equal.

Try this: try 15° and 30°, then go all the way to 90°.

What happened?

Adam gets steadily slower as the angle grows: about 108 steps at 15°, about 181 at 30°. At 90° it's back to 43, because the valley lines up with an axis again, just the other one.

7. How sensitive is each one to the learning rate?

Tick Same learning rate for all and set it to 0.02 on the 100:1 valley. Press Play.

What happened?

Gradient descent and momentum both blow up. Adam is fine. Because Adam divides each coordinate by the size of its own gradient, its steps are roughly $\eta$ in size no matter how steep the ground is. That makes it much harder to pick a learning rate that breaks it. It also means "the same learning rate" isn't a fair comparison: the same number means something completely different for each optimizer.

8. What this picture doesn't show

This page is built to give you intuition, not to rank optimizers.

  • Real networks have millions of parameters, not two. Their valleys aren't neat bowls lined up with an axis or rotated by an exact angle.
  • Adam isn't always the best, and it isn't always the worst. Which optimizer works better depends on the problem, and on tuning the learning rate for each one.
  • Our landscape has a single minimum. Real ones have many, and which one an optimizer lands in matters too. A landscape with two minima would be a good next step for this explainer.

9. Playground

Everything is unlocked here. Change anything you like. If you want ideas, start with these:

Idea 1: set the valley to 20:1 and rotate it to 45°.

What happened?

Adam barely slows down this time (about 43 steps to about 54). Rotation only hurts Adam a lot when the valley is very narrow. In a round bowl, the angle wouldn't matter at all.

Idea 2: gradient descent alone on 100:1. Try a learning rate of 0.004, then 0.0095.

What happened?

At 0.004 the path is smooth, with no zig-zag, but so slow it may not finish in 300 steps. At 0.0095 it zig-zags hard but finishes faster. For gradient descent, the fastest learning rate sits right next to the one that blows up.

Idea 3: Adam alone at 45°. Press Step a few times and read the effective learning rate per coordinate in the right panel.

What happened?

The two numbers are nearly equal. That's the whole story of section 6 in two numbers: Adam can only treat directions differently when they line up with different coordinates.

Idea 4: all three, rotation 45°, then switch the valley from 20:1 to 50:1 to 100:1.

What happened?

Gradient descent gets much slower as the valley narrows (about 47, then 119, then 240 steps), because its speed limit is set by the steepest direction. Adam slows down almost as badly (about 54, 118, 208). Momentum barely changes (about 59 to 64). In a narrow, turned valley, momentum is the one that holds up best.
